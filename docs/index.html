How vague is this?

The aim is to be as vague as the data, no more, no less. Here is what that means, and what the map can't tell you.

Where the borders come from

Every shape comes from historical-basemaps, a GPL-3.0 dataset curated by André Ourednik with outside contributions, compiled from many older maps of mixed quality. It calls itself work in progress. It does not record which source each border was drawn from, or by whom. The map uses one fixed version (commit 4b5039e), simplified for the web to about 4% of its points, which loses fine detail along edges and coasts. 325 slivers of 0.2 km² or less vanished in that step; two names disappear entirely with them.

What a snapshot is

There are 54 snapshots, from 123,000 BCE to 2010 CE, spaced very unevenly: 113,000 years between the first two, two years between 1878 and 1880. Each is a point sample, not an average and not a record of everything that happened between it and the next. The dataset doesn't say how exact a snapshot's date is. Nothing is drawn in between: when a border jumps, the jump is the honest picture. The era names on the timeline are conventional, Eurasia-centred labels for orientation, not data.

Soft and crisp edges

The dataset flags each border as 1 (approximate), 2 (moderately precise) or 3 (determined by international law). Only four borders use 2, so it's effectively a yes/no flag; 2 is drawn as approximate, rounding toward uncertainty. Approximate borders are blurred in proportion to their size; the others are drawn crisp. Nothing is blurred for decoration.

In practice the flag tracks the date more than the border: every snapshot up to 1600 is approximate (bar three shapes), and from 1650 on nearly everything is crisp, right after the 1648 Peace of Westphalia, which the dataset's own notes name as when national borders start to mean something in Europe. That cut-off is applied to the whole world. A crisp edge in 1700 means "the dataset treats this as a legal border", not "this line was well known on the ground".

Mostly peoples, not just states

This is closer to an atlas of who lived roughly where than a chart of empires. The longest-lived entries are hunter-gatherer, farming and pastoral peoples. 41% of all shapes have no name at all (islands, unclaimed land); they stay faint grey and unlabelled.

Kind is shown only where something says so. Of the 10,399 named shapes, 73% are not classified: nothing in the data says whether they were a state or a people, and the map doesn't guess. About 26% get their kind from a hand-made identity table (284 rows, drafted with an AI assistant and being reviewed), and under 1% from the dataset's own rare "type" field. There is no "empire" category, because nothing in the data separates an empire from any other state. Where the dataset's shapes overlap, they're drawn overlapping.

Colour follows identity. The identity table groups some names into one lineage (Roman Republic → Roman Empire → Byzantium keep one colour); every other name gets its own colour, so a change of name means a change of colour, which is not a claim that anything else changed. Labels always show the dataset's own name for that snapshot.

The projection

The map is an azimuthal equidistant projection centred on the North Pole and cut off at 60°S. Distances and directions from the pole are true; everything else stretches the further south it is, so South America, southern Africa and Australia look wider than they are, and Europe sits in the middle by choice, not importance. Antarctica isn't shown.

Links

A "curated link" was chosen by hand and points to the article for the whole lineage (Byzantium links to "Roman Empire"). A "machine-matched" link is an article whose title is exactly the name, which can still be about something else of that name. Otherwise there's no link rather than a guess. Opening a panel fetches the summary from en.wikipedia.org.

What is not shown

  • anything between snapshots, or how borders moved;
  • anything south of 60°S;
  • who drew each border, and from what source;
  • populations, languages, religions, trade or movement;
  • disputed claims, except where the dataset draws them as overlapping shapes;
  • past coastlines, rivers and lakes (the shapes mostly follow today's coasts, even in the Ice Age);
  • labels that don't fit their shape (hover or click it instead).
